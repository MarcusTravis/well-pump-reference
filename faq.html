What size well pump do I need?

Size a pump from two numbers: the flow you need in gallons per minute (gpm), and the total dynamic head (TDH) in feet the pump must work against at that flow. A common starting point is about 1 gpm per water fixture in the house, plus any irrigation zone that runs at the same time; a typical 3-bedroom, 2-bath home often lands around 6 to 12 gpm. TDH is the lift from the pumping water level, plus the switch’s cut-out pressure × 2.31, plus pipe friction loss. Then pick a pump whose official curve delivers your gpm at your TDH, and never more gpm than the well can recover. Horsepower follows from the curve, not the other way round. Worked examples are on Sizing basics; have a licensed installer confirm.

Should I get a submersible pump or a jet pump?

Use a jet pump when the water is shallow (within about 25 ft of the pump), or a convertible jet with a down-well ejector for a moderately deep well. It sits above ground, so it is easy to reach and service. Use a submersible for deeper wells and higher flow: it sits under water in the casing and pushes water up, so suction limits do not apply, and it runs quietly out of sight. The trade-off is that servicing a submersible means pulling it out of the well. This library lists 101 submersibles and 58 jet pumps.

What is the difference between a shallow-well and a deep-well jet pump?

A shallow-well jet has its ejector built into the pump body and lifts water by suction through a single pipe, which limits it to about 25 ft of lift. A deep-well (convertible) jet puts the ejector down in the well and uses two pipes: one sends driving water down, the other brings it back up with the well water. That reaches deeper water, but flow falls as depth rises. Convertible pumps can be set up either way, and the ejector or jet kit is often sold separately. For example, Wayne lists its CWS50 convertible jet for deep-well use to 90 ft with a separate jet assembly.

How deep can a jet pump pull water?

About 25 ft for a shallow-well jet. Suction is limited by air pressure: at sea level, atmospheric pressure (about 14.7 psi) can hold up a column of water roughly 34 ft tall at best, and pipe friction, altitude and warmer water cut that down, so about 25 ft is the practical limit. The shallow-well jets listed here from Goulds, Red Lion, Flint & Walling and Wayne are rated for lift to 25 ft. Deeper than that, use a deep-well jet with a down-well ejector or a submersible.

What is a constant-pressure well pump system?

A constant-pressure system uses a variable-frequency drive (VFD) to change the motor’s speed so pressure stays near one set point, instead of swinging between a switch’s cut-in and cut-out (for example 40 and 60 psi). You get steadier pressure when several taps run, soft motor starts, and a much smaller pressure tank (a small one is still needed). Downsides are a higher price and a drive that must be matched to the motor and installed per the maker’s manual. Compare 30 systems and drives.

What size pressure tank do I need?

Size the tank by drawdown, the usable water between cut-in and cut-out, not by its total gallons. Pump and tank makers generally ask for at least about one minute of pump run time per cycle, so you want drawdown of roughly your pump’s gpm × 1 minute. At a 40/60 psi setting, drawdown is about a quarter of total tank volume, so a 10 gpm pump points to a tank of about 40 gallons or more. Check the maker’s own drawdown table for your setting. What should the pressure tank air precharge be?

About 2 psi below the pump’s cut-in pressure: 28 psi for a 30/50 switch, 38 psi for a 40/60 switch. Check it with the pump’s power off and the water pressure drained to zero, using a tire gauge on the tank’s air valve.

Should I set my pressure switch to 30/50 or 40/60 psi?

Both are common factory settings with a 20 psi spread: the pump starts at the lower number and stops at the higher one. 40/60 gives stronger pressure at the taps, but the pump must reach 10 psi more (about 23 ft more head), and the same tank gives slightly less drawdown. Before raising the setting, check on the pump’s official curve that it can reach the new cut-out at your well’s depth, and reset the tank precharge to 2 psi below the new cut-in. Adjust only with the power off, following the switch maker’s instructions. Browse switches in Controls.

What is the difference between a 2-wire and a 3-wire submersible pump?

A 2-wire submersible has its starting parts inside the motor, so it needs no control box: two power wires plus a ground run down the well. A 3-wire motor keeps its starting parts (capacitor and relay) in a control box above ground, with three power wires plus a ground. The control box can be serviced without pulling the pump, and 3-wire motors are offered in larger sizes. A 3-wire motor needs the control box matched to its brand, horsepower and voltage. Both are single-phase; three-phase motors use different starters.

Should my well pump be 115 V or 230 V?

Match the pump to the supply at the well. For the same power, a 230 V motor draws about half the current of a 115 V motor, so it can use smaller wire over a long run down the well, which is why most submersibles are 230 V. In this library, the 115 V submersibles are all 1/2 HP or smaller, while every jet pump listed is dual-voltage 115/230 V and is switched by a wiring change described in its manual. Have an electrician check wire size, voltage drop and the breaker.

How many people in the US get water from a private well?

The U.S. Geological Survey estimates that around 43 million people in the United States get household water from private domestic wells, which the household owns and maintains rather than a utility (USGS).

Can I install a well pump myself?

This site is a reference, not installation advice. Well pump work involves line-voltage wiring, heavy equipment over an open well, and a risk of contaminating the water supply, and local rules may require a licensed contractor. Confirm your well’s depth, water level, recovery rate, casing size and electrical supply with a licensed well or pump installer and an electrician before buying or installing anything.
